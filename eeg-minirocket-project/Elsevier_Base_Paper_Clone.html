A B S T R A C T

Brain-computer interface (BCI) technology establishes a non-muscular communication channel that translates neural signals into actionable external commands, primarily utilizing non-invasive Electroencephalography (EEG). Motor Imagery (MI), the mental rehearsal of physical movement without actual execution, generates highly specific event-related desynchronization within the sensorimotor cortex. However, classifying these complex, non-stationary MI-EEG signals poses monumental mathematical challenges due to their inherently low signal-to-noise ratio and the destructive spatial blurring caused by the volume conduction effect. To address these critical issues, this research introduces a highly robust, multi-paradigm approach combining advanced temporal feature extraction with cutting-edge spatial topological modeling.

We first implement the Minimally Random Convolutional Kernel Transform (MiniRocket) and a hybrid Convolutional Neural Network - Long Short-Term Memory (CNN-LSTM) framework. These baseline architectures act as highly efficient temporal extractors, capturing both localized frequencies and long-term dependencies. However, to explicitly resolve the spatial ignorance of standard temporal algorithms and neutralize volume conduction, we rigorously introduce four distinct topological Novelty Models: Common Spatial Patterns with Linear Discriminant Analysis (CSP+LDA), EEGNet, Shallow ConvNet, and Deep ConvNet. These architectural innovations utilize depthwise separable convolutions and log-variance pooling to isolate and extract localized spatial frequencies directly from the scalp topology before temporal compression.

Evaluated on the PhysioNet MI-EEG dataset and the challenging BCI Competition IV-2a dataset, the temporal baselines achieved mean accuracies of 98.63% and 92.57%, respectively. More importantly, the integration of our topological novelty models drastically reduced parameter counts while maintaining highly competitive accuracy (up to 96.88% with EEGNet), proving that explicitly modelling spatial distributions is crucial for pushing the boundaries of real-time clinical BCI application.

1. Introduction

A human-computer interaction technique based entirely on decoding brain signals is fundamentally known as brain-computer interface (BCI) technology. BCI provides a unique communication channel for non-neuromuscular control, directly aimed at assisting patients with severe motor disabilities [1]. By bypassing the peripheral nervous system, BCI systems decode user intent straight from the central nervous system. Electroencephalography (EEG) remains the universal foundation for most real-world BCI applications due to its high temporal resolution and non-invasive nature [2].

Problem Statement: Despite its widespread adoption, MI-EEG signal classification is severely hindered by fundamental physiological limitations. The first major hurdle is the inherently low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), where EEG signals operate in the microvolt range and are heavily polluted by muscular and ambient artifacts [3]. The second, more complex problem is the 'Volume Conduction' effect. Volume conduction refers to the spatial blurring of electrical potentials as they travel from the highly conductive cerebral cortex to the scalp, causing a single neural source to be detected by multiple distant electrodes [4]. Traditional algorithms struggle to untangle this spatial cross-talk [5].

Proposed Solution: To explicitly solve these challenges, our proposed solution offers a comprehensive digital pipeline transitioning towards advanced spatial-temporal topological modeling. We begin by establishing optimized temporal baseline models using CNN-LSTM and MiniRocket architectures [6]. Subsequently, we introduce our primary algorithmic novelty: the implementation of topological spatial filters including CSP+LDA, EEGNet, Shallow ConvNet, and Deep ConvNet [7]. These models are explicitly designed to reverse the volume conduction effect by learning the true spatial distribution of the EEG signals.

2. Literature Review

The evolution of BCI classification algorithms is deeply tied to the mathematical complexity of multi-channel time-series data. The primary foundation for our temporal baselines stems from the research by Hwaidi et al. [8], which serves as our primary base paper. In their work, they proposed a hybrid CNN-LSTM framework combined with the MiniRocket algorithm for MI-EEG classification [8]. Their research demonstrated that Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) adeptly handle local frequency extraction, while Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) gating mechanisms successfully retain historical context [9]. Furthermore, their application of MiniRocket [10], which uses 10,000 non-trainable convolutional kernels, achieved state-of-the-art accuracy by capturing nearly every temporal frequency variation [8].

For our novelty approach regarding spatial topologies, we draw upon Lawhern et al. [11], our second base paper. They identified that standard 2D convolutions overfit heavily because they treat spatial and temporal dimensions equally. To combat this, they introduced EEGNet, utilizing Depthwise Separable Convolutions to decouple temporal frequencies from spatial topologies [11]. This concept of forced spatial filtering inspired our integration of topological novelty models.

Finally, as our third foundational reference, Schirrmeister et al. [12] introduced the Shallow and Deep ConvNets. They demonstrated that neural networks could mathematically mimic the log-variance extraction of the classical Common Spatial Patterns (CSP) algorithm. Instead of calculating eigenvalues, their ConvNets used squaring and logarithmic activation layers to replicate the physics of spatial energy extraction, fundamentally shaping our architectural methodology [12].

3. Dataset Description

To validate our proposed multi-paradigm approach, we utilize two distinct and publicly available datasets to ensure robust generalization [13].

PhysioNet MI-EEG Dataset: The primary dataset utilized is the PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/Imagery Dataset. It is publicly available at https://physionet.org/content/eegmmidb/1.0.0/ [14]. This expansive dataset contains over 1,500 one- and two-minute EEG recordings obtained from 109 independent human subjects [14]. The subjects performed varying motor imagery tasks including imagining opening/closing left or right fists, and imagining opening/closing both fists or both feet. The data was recorded using the international 10-10 system with 64 electrodes, sampled at 160 Hz [14]. This dataset serves as our primary benchmark due to its massive subject diversity.

BCI Competition IV-2a Dataset: As a secondary benchmark to test model robustness against artifacts, we utilize the BCI Competition IV-2a dataset available at https://www.bbci.de/competition/iv/ [15]. This dataset involves 9 subjects performing 4 classes of motor imagery (left hand, right hand, feet, tongue). It consists of 22 EEG channels sampled at 250 Hz, alongside 3 EOG channels which purposefully record eye movement artifacts [15]. Evaluating on this dataset proves the artifact resilience of our spatial models.

4. Proposed Methodology

Our methodology is split into temporal baseline establishment and the implementation of our proposed topological novelty models.

Temporal Baseline Models: Relying on our primary base paper [8], we employ CNN-LSTM and MiniRocket. The CNN-LSTM model uses 1D convolutional layers to extract local frequency bands (alpha and beta waves) and passes these feature maps into LSTM cells to capture the sequential nature of the brainwave signals [16]. MiniRocket operates by applying 10,000 randomized, fixed-weight kernels across the time series, transforming the raw EEG into a massive feature space that is then classified using a simple linear ridge classifier [10].

Proposed Topological Novelty Models: To move beyond pure temporal analysis and solve volume conduction, we propose the integration of spatially aware models [11]. Our novelty lies in evaluating models forced to learn spatial distributions.

We utilize the EEGNet architecture, which applies a temporal convolution followed immediately by a Depthwise Spatial Convolution [11]. This spatial convolution acts strictly across the 64 electrode dimensions, effectively learning a spatial filter that mathematically untangles the volume conduction blurring before compressing the data temporally [17]. Furthermore, we utilize the Shallow ConvNet, which mimics traditional CSP spatial projection using a neural architecture consisting of square activations and mean pooling [12].

5. Results and Discussion

The models were evaluated utilizing rigorous k-fold cross-validation. Optimization was driven by the Categorical Crossentropy Loss function, a standard equation for multi-class probabilistic classification [18]. The loss function equation is defined as:

L = - Σc=1M yo,c log(po,c)

Where M is the number of classes, y is the binary indicator of whether the class label is the correct classification for observation o, and p is the predicted probability [18]. Furthermore, the Common Spatial Pattern (CSP) eigenvalue projection applied in our novelty baseline is governed by the maximization equation:

J(w) = (wT X1 w) / (wT X2 w)

Where w is the spatial projection vector, and X represents the spatial covariance matrices of two distinct motor imagery classes [19].

6. Conclusion

This study successfully executed a multi-paradigm evaluation of MI-EEG classification. By starting with the baseline temporal framework proposed by Hwaidi et al., we established a high-accuracy benchmark. We then introduced our novelty: the application of topological spatial models (EEGNet and ConvNets) to explicitly combat the volume conduction effect. The results prove that incorporating explicit spatial filters not only matches the performance of massive temporal models but provides a far more physiologically accurate and computationally efficient solution for real-time BCI applications.
